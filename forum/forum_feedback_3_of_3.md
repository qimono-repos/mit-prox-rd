## Reply (ready to paste)

[NAME], your qualification is fair for today's hardware, and I would add one angle: a low qubit count is not only a limitation, it is also a classroom. Error rates and coherence decide what a machine can compute; they do not decide what a student can learn. Two or three qubits are enough to watch superposition, interference and a Deutsch–Jozsa circuit run on real hardware, not only in a simulator.

That is why Argentina's universities started small. UNAHUR (University National of the City Hurlingham, Buenos Aires) installed a 3-qubit NMR SpinQ, the first quantum computer in Argentina's public university system, and leads the [Iniciativa Cuántica](https://unahur.edu.ar/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Declaracion-de-Intencion-para-la-promocion-y-desarrollo-de-una-iniciativa-cuantica.pdf) with UNICEN (University National of the Center of the Province of Buenos Aires), UNRN (University National of Río Negro), UNLP (University National of La Plata) and others ([press coverage](https://www.lagaceta.com.ar/nota/1117347/sociedad/universidad-publica-suma-computacion-cuantica-para-sirve-clave.html)). For local development I wrote to SpinQ directly, and they quoted me USD 12,000 for a 2-qubit machine. Small machines lower the entry barrier.

On measurable advantage, I think qubit count is the wrong scoreboard. Google's [Willow](https://blog.google/technology/research/google-willow-quantum-chip/) went below the error-correction threshold, so the real race is gate depth at low logical error. The same yardstick applies to Microsoft's [Majorana 2](https://news.microsoft.com/source/features/innovation/majorana-2-microsoft-discovery-agentic-ai/): about 20 seconds of parity lifetime is impressive, but parity lifetime is not coherence.

That is why I still believe Gil: he promised a path, not an arrival date, and the people who will walk it are being trained on small machines today. Thanks for raising the bar on rigor in this discussion.
