# Quantum Computing: Big Investment, Bigger Questions
## Draft Version Six — Corrected

The original article shows quantum technology emerging as both a scientific frontier and a geopolitical contest. China built a Beijing–Shanghai quantum network, while South Korea, Britain, and Australia pursued their own initiatives. The United States also matters because of its research ecosystem and government access to intelligence and technical reports. Quantum networks may strengthen secure communications, while raising questions about technological sovereignty and international trust.

In financial services, the Commonwealth Bank of Australia’s research partnership with Michelle Simmons’s team at the University of New South Wales illustrates how quantum computing could reach beyond physics. Portfolio optimization is one potential application, alongside other complex financial problems.

Investment in quantum start-ups has risen sharply. McKinsey’s Quantum Technology Monitor documents the growing flow of capital into the sector, reflecting expectations around future commercial returns, scientific discovery, optimization, and the strategic need to develop expertise before more mature hardware becomes available. Companies and governments are investing not only in machines, but in software, skills, research partnerships, and the ability to recognize where quantum methods may be useful.

This is where “a solution in search of a problem” becomes a productive question. AI offers a comparison, but not a prophecy: tools such as AlphaFold have already made more than 200 million predicted protein structures available to researchers. Quantum computing follows a different path, with particular promise in quantum chemistry, materials science, and selected computational problems. The challenge is to discover and develop applications that make use of its distinctive capabilities.

As a person born in South America I can give this personal experience in Argentina. Argentina has interesting projects, including CNEA research on an experimental processor using superconducting fluxonium qubits. At the same time, national science-and-technology budget analyses report that funding has reached a historic low in the long-running series. This is an Argentina-no-continuity problem: advanced research requires sustained support for people, equipment, and infrastructure, not only promising projects.

In software, Quantum as a Service (QaaS) offers access to quantum processors through cloud platforms, while hybrid setups combine classical computing with quantum hardware for selected tasks. With organizations already expanding AI and cloud infrastructure, QaaS may lower the friction of experimentation. Quantum software connects theory with practice through programming tools, algorithms, simulation, and resource estimation.

I partly agree with IBM’s Dario Gil: quantum computing may help researchers approach problems that conventional computers struggle to solve. Software helps identify those problems, formulate algorithms, and test approaches. Quantum supremacy is one milestone in this evolving idea, while the longer story is how quantum technology becomes part of science, engineering, and industry.
