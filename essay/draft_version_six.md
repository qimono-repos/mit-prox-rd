# Quantum Computing: Big Investment, Bigger Questions
## Draft Version Six

The original article shows quantum technology emerging as both a scientific frontier and a geopolitical contest. China built a Beijing–Shanghai quantum network, while South Korea, Britain, and Australia pursued their own initiatives. The United States also matters because of its research ecosystem and government access to intelligence and technical reports. Quantum networks may strengthen secure communications, but they also raise questions about technological sovereignty and international trust.

In financial services, the Commonwealth Bank of Australia’s research partnership with Michelle Simmons’s team at the University of New South Wales illustrates how quantum computing could reach beyond physics. Portfolio optimization is one potential application, alongside other complex financial problems. The central question is which problems genuinely benefit from quantum computation, rather than simply being interesting to test.

Investment momentum helps explain why organizations are entering the field now. McKinsey’s Quantum Technology Monitor reported that private investment in quantum start-ups rose sharply, reaching a new high in 2025. That signals investor confidence, but capital flowing into a technology is not proof that useful quantum advantage has arrived. The four motivations are potential early commercial returns, scientific discovery in chemistry and materials, optimization applications, and the strategic need to develop expertise before mature hardware becomes available.

This is where “a solution in search of a problem” becomes a useful challenge rather than a verdict. Compare quantum computing with AI in science: AI systems such as AlphaFold have already produced a widely used resource of more than 200 million predicted protein structures. Quantum computing has exciting prospects in chemistry and materials science, but its practical value must be established problem by problem. AI’s impact does not guarantee that quantum computing will follow the same trajectory.

As a person born in South America I can give this personal experience in Argentina. Argentina has interesting projects, including CNEA research on an experimental processor using superconducting fluxonium qubits. Yet national science-and-technology budget analyses report a historic low in the long-running series, with the latest years following a period of sustained cuts. This is an Argentina-no-continuity problem: advanced research requires continuity in people, equipment, and infrastructure, not only promising projects.

In software, Quantum as a Service (QaaS) offers access to quantum processors through cloud platforms, while hybrid setups combine classical computing with quantum hardware for selected tasks. With organizations already expanding AI and cloud infrastructure, QaaS may lower the friction of experimentation, though costs and performance still matter. Quantum software builds the bridge between theory and practical tests through programming tools, algorithms, simulation, and resource estimation.

I partly agree with IBM’s Dario Gil: quantum computing may help us approach problems beyond practical classical capabilities, but not universally and not automatically. Quantum supremacy makes headlines; repeatable advantage on meaningful problems is the more demanding test. Government strategy, corporate partnerships, and software development will help determine whether quantum technology becomes a useful tool or remains an expensive promise.
