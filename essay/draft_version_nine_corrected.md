# Quantum Computing: Big Investment, Bigger Questions
## Draft Version Nine, Corrected

### 1. Quantum networks and global relations

In Jason Palmer’s 2017 *Economist* article, countries approach quantum networks through different combinations of security, research ambition, and technological sovereignty. China’s Beijing–Shanghai network and satellite-linked experiments signal a large, state-backed infrastructure ambition; efforts in Europe, Australia, South Korea, and the United States also connect quantum communication to national research and security priorities.[^1] Since 2017, quantum networks have become more than a scientific curiosity: they sit at the intersection of secure communications, strategic competition, and international research cooperation. My conviction is that they should complement classical networks, not replace them. Their strongest roles are likely to be carefully selected government communications, critical infrastructure, finance, and high-value scientific collaboration. International standards and cooperation matter because secure communications should not become another reason to make scientific exchange impossible.

### 2. Why invest now? Four reasons

The article’s investment case can be organized into four reasons. First, early and smaller machines may find commercial uses before fully fault-tolerant quantum computers exist. Second, quantum simulation could help researchers understand chemistry and materials, with implications for batteries, industrial processes, and medicine. Third, quantum methods may eventually help with selected optimization problems in logistics and financial services, including portfolio optimization. Fourth, investing early builds the software, algorithms, engineering skills, research partnerships, and strategic capability needed when the hardware improves. Security adds urgency for governments, particularly as they prepare for a future in which powerful quantum computers could threaten some current public-key cryptography.[^2]

I find scientific discovery and early capability-building the most convincing reasons. New materials and better chemical simulations could create value beyond the quantum industry itself. And expertise cannot be summoned overnight when hardware matures. The evolving idea of what quantum computers are useful for is still being explored, which makes learning and experimentation important now. That uncertainty also makes “a solution in search of a problem” a useful question to keep asking: which concrete scientific or industrial problems are best matched to quantum methods, and how will we recognize a meaningful result?

### 3. Quantum software and Dario Gil’s claim

Dario Gil argues that quantum computing offers a path toward problems conventional computers cannot solve. Quantum software helps pursue that ambition in three ways: it provides programming tools and languages to express quantum operations; it translates real problems into quantum algorithms; and it uses classical simulation, resource estimation, and hybrid workflows to test whether an approach is feasible. Software is the bridge between abstract quantum theory and an experiment that can be inspected, compared, and improved.

I believe Gil’s statement is directionally persuasive, not because every difficult problem will become solvable by a quantum computer, but because quantum computing may expand the set of tractable problems in specific areas. The practical work is identifying those problems and designing algorithms that use quantum effects meaningfully. AI may also help scientists and engineers develop algorithms and notice questions they had not previously formulated. For comparison, AlphaFold’s database already contains more than 200 million predicted protein structures, a visible example of AI-enabled scientific infrastructure; quantum computing’s application landscape is at a different stage.[^4] Classical computing remains essential.

### 4. When physics becomes everyone’s business

Palmer’s examples show quantum concepts moving into corporate engineering: Intel explored silicon qubits; Microsoft pursued quantum hardware and software; IBM opened cloud access to quantum systems; and Bosch and Airbus investigated quantum simulation for batteries and advanced materials. The article also describes governments supporting research and quantum communication because of the technology’s strategic implications.[^1] The message behind “subjects that used to be mere footnotes to physics will rule” is that quantum literacy will increasingly matter to people who build products, software, infrastructure, and policy, not only physicists.

A current example makes this shift concrete. Canonical’s Ubuntu 26.10, released in October 2026, introduces post-quantum-ready package signing, using Sequoia PGP to verify APT repository signatures; Ubuntu 26.10 also integrates support for post-quantum algorithms including ML-DSA and ML-KEM in its cryptographic ecosystem.[^5] This is not a quantum computer running Ubuntu. It is conventional software infrastructure adapting now to the future security risks posed by quantum-capable attackers. The distinction matters: post-quantum cryptography runs on ordinary computers but is designed to withstand attacks that sufficiently powerful quantum computers could enable.

Digital signatures deserve particular attention. They authenticate who signed software or data and help detect tampering. NIST has standardized ML-DSA (FIPS 204) and SLH-DSA (FIPS 205) as post-quantum digital-signature schemes.[^6] Package signing is therefore a useful everyday example of quantum theory affecting the trust chain beneath software installation and updates. It also illustrates why the phrase “speak quantum” should not mean that every engineer must become a physicist. It means technical professionals need enough quantum literacy to understand where the risks are, what standards are changing, and how to make sound implementation choices.

As a person born in South America I can give this personal experience in Argentina. CNEA is pursuing an experimental processor using superconducting fluxonium qubits, including the FLUXOPRO project, while Argentina’s national science-and-technology budget has faced severe cuts.[^3] I call this Argentina-no-continuity: promising research exists, but sustained support for people, equipment, and infrastructure is fragile. In my own work across enterprise technology and AI, I see why technical leaders need enough quantum literacy to evaluate real capabilities rather than merely repeat the hype. Quantum as a Service (QaaS) and hybrid setups may lower the barrier to learning by letting teams experiment through cloud platforms while continuing to use classical computing.
